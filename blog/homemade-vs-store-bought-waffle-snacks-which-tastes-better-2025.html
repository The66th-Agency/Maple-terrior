Homemade vs Store-Bought Stroopwafels: Time, Tools and Taste

By Maple Terroir April 30, 2025 3 min read

Our maple stroopwafels come ready to eat. Making them at home takes a waffle iron, a candy thermometer and most of an afternoon. This guide sets out what each route involves, so you can decide which one suits you.

For anyone ready to take on the scratch version, the complete Dutch stroopwafel recipe covers every step from dough to caramel filling.

Time: Most of an Afternoon, or None

Stroopwafels are a yeasted cookie, so the dough needs time. You mix it, divide it into 50 g balls and let them rest in a warm spot for 45 to 60 minutes before baking [2]. While the dough rests, you cook the filling to the soft-ball stage, 234°F to 240°F on a candy thermometer.

Then the waffles bake, about 2 to 3 minutes each on the iron, and each one has to be split into two thin rounds straight away, while it is still hot [1]. You fill it, press the halves back together and start the next one. The Baking Sense recipe makes 18 stroopwafels, so a batch keeps you at the iron for 36 to 54 minutes, and the washing up comes after.

Ours arrive baked and filled. The maple stroopwafel caddy holds individually wrapped 34 g stroopwafels, and our mini stroopwafels come 15 to a 128 g box.

Tools: What You Need at Home

A stroopwafel iron makes the thinnest waffles, and a pizzelle or waffle cone maker works as a substitute [1]. You also need a candy thermometer for the filling, a thin, sharp knife for splitting the waffles, and a stand mixer helps with the dough [2]. If you do not own an iron, the first batch costs more than the ingredients.

Taste and Ingredients

Baking at home lets you choose every ingredient, and a stroopwafel split and filled a few minutes ago is still warm. Classic recipes fill them with golden syrup or Dutch stroop, and some bakers use dark maple syrup instead for a maple filling.

Our stroopwafels list Canadian-grown flour as the first ingredient, along with butter, eggs, cane sugar, and pure maple syrup in the filling. To warm one, rest it on top of a cup of hot coffee or tea and let the steam soften the filling.

Storage

Homemade or bought, stroopwafels keep best in a closed container in a dry, dark place. Keep them away from warm spots, because heat can make the filling leak [3]. The stroopwafels in our caddy are individually wrapped, so you can take one with you.

Which to Choose

If you enjoy a baking project and own the tools, the homemade route is worth an afternoon. If you want stroopwafels with your coffee today, our maple stroopwafels are ready to eat.
