Maple Glazed Ham: A Pure Maple Syrup Glaze for a Holiday Ham

Simmer pure maple syrup with Dijon mustard, cider vinegar, butter and a pinch of cloves for about 8 minutes, until it coats a spoon. Warm a fully cooked ham covered at 325°F (160°C), then brush on the glaze three times over the last 30 minutes in a hotter oven. The glaze takes 10 minutes and serves a 3 to 4 kg (7 to 9 lb) half ham.

By Maple Terroir 8 min read
Published November 7, 2026 Last updated November 7, 2026

One cup of syrup, one ham.The glaze uses ¾ cup (175 mL) of pure maple syrup, most of a 250 mL bottle. Dark syrup gives the deepest maple taste on the ham; Golden gives a lighter, sweeter glaze. Both bottles are ours, from one farm.

A maple glaze for ham is pure maple syrup cooked down with mustard and a little acid until it is thick enough to cling, then brushed onto the ham in layers at the end of baking. The mustard and vinegar keep it from tasting like pancake topping, and the layers build a shiny, dark crust that stays on the slices.

A ham sold as fully cooked or ready to eat is already cooked, so the oven has two jobs: warm the ham through without drying it, then set the glaze. The recipe card below handles both. After it you will find how to tell what kind of ham you bought, how long to warm it by weight, when to brush the glaze on, which maple syrup to use, five ways to change the glaze, and how long leftovers keep.

Maple Glazed Ham

Prep15 min
Cook2 to 2½ hr
Glaze10 min
Serves12 to 14

Ham

  • 3 to 4 kg (7 to 9 lb)fully cooked bone-in half ham, spiral cut or whole
  • ½ cup (125 mL)water or apple cider, for the pan

Maple glaze

  • ¾ cup (175 mL)pure maple syrup, Dark for the strongest flavor
  • 3 tbsp (45 mL)Dijon mustard
  • 1 tbsp (15 mL)apple cider vinegar
  • 2 tbsp (30 g)butter
  • ¼ tspground cloves
  • ¼ tspblack pepper

Method

  1. Take the ham out of the fridge an hour before baking. Heat the oven to 325°F (160°C) with a rack in the lower third.
  2. Set the ham cut side down in a roasting pan, pour the water or cider into the pan and cover the pan tightly with foil. If the ham is not spiral cut, score the fat in a diamond pattern about 5 mm (¼ inch) deep first.
  3. Bake covered until a thermometer in the thickest part, away from the bone, reads about 60°C (140°F), roughly 1½ to 2 hours for a 3.5 kg (8 lb) half ham.
  4. While it bakes, simmer the glaze ingredients in a small saucepan over medium heat, stirring, for 6 to 8 minutes, until reduced to about ¾ cup and thick enough to coat a spoon. Take it off the heat.
  5. Uncover the ham and turn the oven up to 400°F (200°C). Brush on a third of the glaze, working it between the slices, and bake 10 minutes. Repeat twice, until the glaze is dark and lacquered and the center reads 74°C (165°F).
  6. Rest the ham for 15 minutes before carving. Stir the pan juices into any glaze left in the saucepan and serve it on the side.

Tools

  • Roasting pan and foil
  • Instant-read thermometer
  • Small saucepan
  • Pastry brush

Which Ham to Buy

Read the label before anything else, because it decides the cooking. A ham labeled fully cooked or ready to eat only needs warming and glazing, which is what this recipe does. A ham labeled fresh, raw or uncooked has to be cooked through, to 71°C (160°F) in the center, the temperature Health Canada gives for pork, ham included. That takes much longer, so follow the time on its package and add the glaze for the last 30 minutes.

As a guide to size, plan on a 3 to 4 kg (7 to 9 lb) bone-in half ham for 12 to 14 people with some left over. The glaze on the recipe card covers that size; double it for a whole ham.

How Long to Bake It

Go by the thermometer, not the clock. Hams vary in shape, bone and how cold they start, so times are only a starting point. Warm a fully cooked ham covered at 325°F (160°C) until the center reads about 60°C (140°F), then the glazing stage brings it the rest of the way. We finish at 74°C (165°F), the temperature Health Canada gives for reheating cooked food.

The first check is where to start looking, not when to stop. Put the thermometer in the thickest part, away from the bone, because bone conducts heat and reads high.

When to Brush on the Glaze

Glaze only at the end. Maple syrup is mostly sugar, and on a ham that bakes for two hours it would burn long before the meat is warm. Three thin coats in a hotter oven set into a shiny crust; one thick coat slides off into the pan.

Watch the last coat. A glaze that has turned deep amber with a few dark edges is done; black spots mean it is time to take the ham out, whatever the clock says. If the ham is at temperature before the glaze has colored, a few minutes under the broiler on the middle rack finishes it, with the door in sight.

The glaze is ready to brush when it coats the back of a spoon and a line drawn through it holds for a second. If it has thickened too much while it waits, warm it over low heat with a teaspoon of water.

What Each Glaze Ingredient Does

Pure maple syrup

The syrup is the glaze: it thickens as it simmers and caramelizes on the ham. Use pure maple syrup, made only from boiled maple sap. Pancake or table syrup is mostly corn syrup with flavoring, and it turns thin and sticky rather than glossy; our guide to pure maple syrup vs table syrup shows how to read the label. This recipe uses no brown sugar, so the maple is the only sweetness and the ham tastes of it.

Dijon mustard and cider vinegar

Ham is salty and maple is sweet, and the mustard and vinegar sit between them. The mustard also thickens the glaze slightly, so it clings instead of running off.

Butter, cloves and pepper

Butter gives the glaze its shine. Cloves are the traditional holiday ham spice; a quarter teaspoon is enough to taste without numbing. Pepper keeps the finish savory.

Which Maple Syrup to Use

A dark syrup keeps the most maple flavor after it has been reduced and baked, so it is our first choice for a ham glaze. A golden syrup works too and gives a lighter, sweeter glaze. The two differ in color and strength of taste, as our guide to maple syrup grades explains.

Once a bottle is open, keep it in the fridge; the maple syrup shelf life checker says how long it lasts.

Five Ways to Change the Glaze

Keep the maple syrup, mustard and butter, and change one other thing. Each goes into the saucepan with the rest.

  • Orange: the zest of one orange and ¼ cup (60 mL) of its juice in place of the vinegar; simmer 2 minutes longer.
  • Bourbon or rye: 2 tablespoons (30 mL), added with the syrup so the alcohol cooks off while it reduces.
  • Pineapple: ¼ cup (60 mL) of pineapple juice in place of the vinegar, the classic ham pairing.
  • Chili: ½ teaspoon of chili flakes or 1 teaspoon of sriracha, for heat against the sweet.
  • Whole-grain mustard: in place of the Dijon, for a glaze with texture.

What to Serve With It

A glazed ham sits at the center of a Christmas or Easter table, so the sides can be simple and cooked on the stovetop while the oven is busy.

  • Vegetables: our maple glazed carrots cook in one skillet in 25 minutes, and the Dijon version of their glaze matches the ham.
  • Potatoes: scalloped, mashed or roasted, to soak up the pan sauce.
  • Something sharp: a green salad with a vinaigrette, or braised red cabbage, to cut the richness.
  • Dessert: maple fudge made a day ahead, cut into squares for the table.

Storing Leftover Ham

Cooked ham keeps 3 to 4 days in the fridge and 2 to 3 months in the freezer, according to Health Canada's food storage chart, in a fridge at 4°C (40°F) or colder. Carve the meat off the bone before you store it, wrap it well, and keep the bone in the freezer for pea soup.

To reheat slices, warm them covered in a 325°F (160°C) oven with a spoonful of water or leftover glaze, until hot through, 74°C (165°F) by Health Canada's figure for leftovers. Cold, the ham goes straight into sandwiches with a little mustard.

Key Takeaways

  • The glaze: ¾ cup pure maple syrup, 3 tablespoons Dijon, 1 tablespoon cider vinegar, 2 tablespoons butter, cloves and pepper, simmered 6 to 8 minutes.
  • Warm a fully cooked half ham covered at 325°F (160°C) to about 60°C (140°F) in the center.
  • Glaze in three thin coats over the last 30 minutes at 400°F (200°C), and take it out at 74°C (165°F).
  • A fresh, uncooked ham needs 71°C (160°F), per Health Canada.
  • Leftover ham keeps 3 to 4 days in the fridge and 2 to 3 months frozen, per Health Canada.
