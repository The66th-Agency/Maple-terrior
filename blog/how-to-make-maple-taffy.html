How to Make Maple Taffy on Snow

Boil one cup of pure maple syrup to 113°C (235°F), pour it in strips over packed snow and roll each strip onto a stick before it sets. No snow? Finely crushed ice works the same way.

MakesAbout 8 sticks

Maple taffy is pure maple syrup boiled until it is thick enough to set on cold snow, then poured in a ribbon and rolled up on a wooden stick. In French it is tire d'érable, or tire sur la neige, and it is the treat sugar shacks in Quebec are known for each spring. In New England it goes by sugar on snow.

You need one ingredient, a saucepan, a thermometer and something cold to pour onto. The whole recipe takes about 20 minutes, and one 250 mL bottle of syrup makes about eight sticks. Below the recipe card you will find the temperature and why it matters, how to test the syrup without a thermometer, three ways to make it without snow, which grade of syrup to use and how to scale it for a party.

Maple Taffy on Snow

Prep10 min
Cook10 min
Total20 min
MakesAbout 8 sticks

Ingredients

  • 1 cup (250 mL)pure maple syrup, one 250 mL bottle
  • 1 panclean snow, packed firm, in a 23 x 33 cm (9 x 13 inch) baking pan
  • or 1 to 2 kgice, crushed fine, if you have no snow

Plan on about 2 tablespoons of finished taffy per stick.

Method

  1. Pack the snow firmly into the baking pan and smooth the top. Keep it outside or in the freezer while the syrup cooks.
  2. Pour the maple syrup into a heavy saucepan that holds at least 2 L, because the syrup foams up to twice its height as it boils. Clip on a candy or digital thermometer with the tip in the syrup, clear of the bottom.
  3. Boil over medium-high heat, without stirring, until the syrup reads 113°C (235°F), about 10 minutes. Take the pan off the heat straight away.
  4. Pour about 2 tablespoons in a line 10 to 12 cm (4 to 5 inches) long across the snow. Repeat, leaving space between the strips.
  5. Wait 3 to 5 seconds, until the edges firm up. Lay a stick at one end of a strip, press it in and roll it toward the other end so the taffy wraps around the stick. Eat it while it is still soft.

Tools

  • Heavy saucepan, 2 L or larger
  • Candy or digital thermometer
  • Metal spoon or small ladle
  • 23 x 33 cm baking pan
  • 8 wooden craft sticks

Boil, Pour, Roll

The method is four moves, and the only one that needs care is the temperature. Everything else is about speed: the syrup sets on the snow in seconds, so have the sticks ready before you pour.

1

Pack the snow

Fill the pan with clean snow and press it down hard. Loose snow lets the hot syrup sink in and melt a hole instead of sitting on top.

2

Boil to 113°C

No stirring while it boils. The bubbles go from large and watery to small and slow as the syrup thickens. Pull it off the heat at 113°C (235°F).

3

Pour a strip

Two tablespoons in a line about the length of your hand. A metal spoon or a heatproof measuring cup with a spout gives you the most control.

4

Roll it up

After 3 to 5 seconds, press a stick into one end and roll. If it tears, wait a few seconds more. If it is already hard, pour the next strip sooner.

What Temperature for Maple Taffy

Boil maple syrup to 113°C (235°F) for taffy you can roll on a stick. That is the finish temperature for maple on snow in the New York State Maple Confections Notebook from Cornell's maple program, and it sits 13°C (23°F) above the point where water boils at sea level.

Every maple treat is the same syrup boiled to a different point. The longer it boils, the more water leaves, and the firmer it sets as it cools. The chart below shows where taffy sits beside the other confections, from the same Cornell table. The bar is the temperature.

Adjust for your kitchen

Water does not boil at exactly 100°C everywhere. It boils lower at altitude and moves a little with the weather, and thermometers drift. The Cornell guide's advice for maple cream applies here too: measure the temperature at which water boils, on the day, with the thermometer you will use.

Bring a pot of water to a rolling boil and read the thermometer. Add 13°C (23°F) to that number, and that is your taffy temperature. If water boils at 98°C in your kitchen, take the syrup off at 111°C. The Massachusetts Maple Producers Association gives a range of 22 to 28°F (12 to 16°C) above boiling water, and says the higher end makes a stiffer taffy.

Softer or chewier

At 113°C (235°F) the taffy is soft and stretchy and rolls easily. Taken to 115°C (239°F), the temperature Ricardo uses in his maple taffy on snow, it sets firmer and chewier on the stick. Go much past that and the strips harden before you can roll them.

How to Test Maple Taffy Without a Thermometer

A thermometer is the reliable way, but two tests tell you when the syrup is close. Start testing once the bubbles turn small and the syrup coats a spoon in a thick layer.

The snow test

Drip a little syrup onto the snow. Ready syrup sits on top and firms into a soft, pliable strip within a few seconds. If it sinks in and disappears, it needs to boil longer.

The cold water test

Drop a small spoonful into a glass of cold water and wait a moment. Maple from Canada's maple taffy recipe says it is ready when you can roll the chilled syrup into a ball.

Take the pan off the heat while you test, so the syrup does not keep climbing past the point you want.

How to Make Maple Taffy Without Snow

Maple taffy only needs a surface cold enough to set the syrup before crystals form. The Cornell notebook explains that syrup poured onto snow or fine shaved ice thickens so quickly that it sets smooth instead of grainy. Any of these three works.

Crushed ice in a baking pan

Crush 1 to 2 kg (2 to 4 lb) of ice as fine as you can in a food processor or blender, a few cups at a time, and pack it into the baking pan. This is the method in Maple from Canada's recipe. Fine ice works better than cubes, because cubes leave gaps the syrup runs into.

Shaved ice

A shaved-ice machine makes ice with a texture close to snow. Both the Cornell notebook and the Massachusetts producers name shaved ice as the stand-in. Pack it firm in the pan the same way.

Snow saved in the freezer

Scoop clean snow into the pan on a snowy day, cover it and keep it in the freezer until you want it. Fresh snow from away from roads and paths, with the top layer scraped off, is the cleanest to use.

Which Maple Syrup to Use for Taffy

Use pure maple syrup, made only from boiled maple sap. The taste of the taffy is the taste of the syrup, concentrated, so the grade you pick shows. Table syrup is mostly corn syrup with flavoring and will not taste of maple; our guide to pure maple syrup vs table syrup explains how to read the label.

Maple from Canada suggests an amber syrup for a rich taste. Between our two, the Dark bottle gives the strongest maple flavor and the Golden gives the gentlest, so pick by taste. Making Maple Taffy for a Crowd

Allow about 2 tablespoons of finished taffy per stick. Food writer Valerie Lugonja of A Canadian Foodie plans one cup of syrup for six to eight people. Boil one cup at a time: a small batch reaches temperature in about 10 minutes, and it stays hot and pourable while you work through the pan.

For a party, keep spare pans of snow or crushed ice in the freezer and swap them in as the first ones fill. Each batch of syrup takes about 10 minutes on the stove, so the next one is ready by the time the last strip is rolled.

When Maple Taffy Goes Wrong

Nearly every problem comes down to the temperature or the snow. These are the four you are most likely to meet, and how to fix each one without starting over.

It melts into the snow

The syrup did not boil long enough. Put the pan back on the heat and keep going until it reaches 113°C (235°F). Ricardo's recipe gives the same fix: if the taffy is too liquid, continue cooking.

It hardens before you can roll it

The syrup went too hot. Stir in a little water off the heat, as Ricardo suggests, then test a drop on the snow again.

It goes grainy in the pan

Stirring or scraping while the syrup boils starts crystals. Boil it without stirring, and pour from the middle of the pan rather than scraping the sides.

It sinks and leaves a hole

The snow is too loose or too warm. Pack it harder and keep the pan outside or in the freezer until the moment you pour.

The Sugar Shack Tradition

Maple taffy belongs to sugaring season, the few weeks each spring when the sap runs and producers boil it into syrup. At a sugar shack, or cabane à sucre, taffy is made alongside the syrup: poured hot onto clean snow and picked up on a wooden stick.

The New England version comes with its own side dishes. The Massachusetts Maple Producers Association lists sour pickles to cut the sweetness, served with saltines or plain doughnuts. It sounds odd and works well, and it is a good way to keep a group eating more than one stick. Safety and Leftovers

Hot syrup burns

At 113°C, boiling syrup is hotter than boiling water, and it sticks to skin. Let an adult do the boiling and the pouring. Children can stand on the far side of the pan and roll the strips once they are on the snow, which is the part they want to do anyway.

Eat it the same day

Maple taffy is at its best a minute after it is rolled. It does not keep well at room temperature, where it softens and slumps off the stick. To hold it for a few hours, or until the next day, leave the rolled sticks on a tray of ice in the freezer.

Syrup left in the pan

Whatever is left in the pan sets as it cools. Warm it gently with a spoonful of water and pour it over pancakes or ice cream, or soak the pan in hot water and it will dissolve.

Key Takeaways

  • Boil 1 cup (250 mL) of pure maple syrup, without stirring, to 113°C (235°F). It makes about eight sticks.
  • Set the temperature 13°C (23°F) above the point where water boils in your kitchen.
  • Pour 2 tablespoons per strip onto packed snow, wait 3 to 5 seconds, then roll it onto a stick.
  • No snow: use ice crushed fine in a food processor, or shaved ice.
  • Too runny means boil longer; too hard means stir in a little water.
