How to Make Maple Butter From Pure Maple Syrup

Real maple butter has one ingredient. Boil maple syrup to 112 to 113°C, chill it without stirring, then stir until it turns pale and spreadable. Below: that method, a five-minute whipped butter version and the jar we sell when you would rather not stir.

Boil to112 to 113°C

Published November 6, 2026 Last updated November 6, 2026

To make maple butter, boil pure maple syrup to 12 to 13°C (22 to 24°F) above the temperature water boils at, cool it quickly without moving it, then stir it slowly until it turns opaque and thick. The stirring makes billions of tiny sugar crystals, and they turn clear syrup into a pale, smooth spread. Nothing else goes in.

Maple butter is also sold as maple cream or maple spread, and the method here follows the one Cornell's maple program publishes for producers. It takes about an hour, most of it waiting for the syrup to cool. If you came for the other maple butter, softened butter whipped with maple syrup, that recipe is further down and takes five minutes.

Two Things Called Maple Butter

Maple butter, or maple cream

Pure maple syrup, boiled, cooled and stirred. No dairy at all; the name comes from how it spreads.

  • One ingredient
  • Needs a thermometer and about an hour
  • Keeps a month or two in the fridge

Whipped maple butter

Softened dairy butter beaten with maple syrup and a pinch of salt. A flavored butter for warm bread.

  • Three ingredients
  • Five minutes, no cooking
  • Keeps about two weeks in the fridge

Maple Butter (Maple Cream)

CookAbout 15 min
Cool to24°C (75°F)
Stir10 to 30 min
MakesAbout 540 g

Ingredients

  • 2 cups (500 mL)pure maple syrup, Golden grade, two 250 mL bottles
  • ¼ tspbutter, optional, to calm the foam

Leave out the butter and it is maple syrup and nothing else, like the jar we sell.

Method

  1. Bring a pot of water to a rolling boil and read your thermometer. Write the number down; it is your starting point.
  2. Pour the syrup into a heavy saucepan of 3 to 4 L, because it foams to more than twice its height. Add the butter if using. Boil over medium-high heat, without stirring, until it reads 12 to 13°C (22 to 24°F) above your water reading: 112 to 113°C (234 to 236°F) at sea level. This takes about 10 to 15 minutes.
  3. Take it off the heat at once and set the pan in a sink of ice water. Do not stir, move or scrape it. If crystals form on the surface, mist it lightly with water.
  4. Leave it until it reads 24°C (75°F) or lower. Cooler is better: 7 to 13°C (45 to 55°F) gives the smoothest cream.
  5. Stir slowly and steadily with a wooden spoon. It loosens first, then thickens, turns pale and loses its gloss: 10 to 30 minutes.
  6. When it is opaque and spreadable, like soft peanut butter, spoon it into clean jars straight away and refrigerate.

Tools

  • Heavy saucepan, 3 to 4 L
  • Digital or candy thermometer
  • Sink or large bowl of ice water
  • Wooden spoon
  • Clean glass jars

The Temperature That Makes Maple Butter

Cornell's maple program says to heat the syrup to 22 to 24°F (12 to 13°C) above the boiling point of water, and to boil to the higher end on rainy or humid days. Its guide, published in the New York State Maple Confections Notebook, stresses measuring the boiling point of water on the day you cook, because it changes with the weather and with altitude.

At sea level that puts maple butter at 112 to 113°C (234 to 236°F). Our own jar is boiled to 112°C. A degree or two lower and the cream stays runny and separates in the jar; a few degrees higher and you are making maple candy. The chart shows how close together the maple confections sit.

Why it has to cool first

Boiled to that point, the syrup holds more sugar than it can keep dissolved once it cools. Cornell explains that if you stir it while it is warm, a few large crystals form and the spread turns grainy and separates sooner. Cooled, still, to 24°C (75°F) or below before stirring, it forms a great many small crystals instead, and those stay small in the jar for weeks.

That is also why you should not stir it, move it or scrape it while it cools. Any knock can start crystals early.

What Stirring Looks Like

Stir slowly and keep going. Cornell's instructions for stirring by hand say not to beat or whip it, and to expect it to take some time and strength. These are the four changes you will see, in order.

1

Thick and glossy

Cold syrup, heavy and clear. Hard work to move at first.

2

Suddenly looser

It thins and warms as crystals start to form. That heat comes from the crystals forming, and it means it is working.

3

Pale and thicker

It lightens in color and stiffens with every turn of the spoon.

4

Opaque and matte

The gloss is gone and it holds a soft shape. Stop here and jar it.

In the maple cream recipe at The View From Great Island, this takes 10 to 30 minutes with a wooden spoon. That recipe advises stirring by hand rather than with a mixer, because the texture changes fast near the end. Cornell suggests two people: one to hold the bowl and one to stir.

Which Maple Syrup to Use

Use a light syrup. Cornell's guide says maple cream is generally made from light or medium colored syrup, because what decides whether it creams is the syrup's invert sugar, the share of its sugar already split into glucose and fructose. The guide recommends 0.5 to 3 percent, which home cooks cannot measure, so a light syrup is the safest bet.

Every bottle of Canada Grade A syrup starts in the same range: the Canadian Grade Compendium sets its sugar content at 66 to 68.9 percent. Boiling to 112°C takes it to about 80 percent, the level Cornell gives for maple cream. That is where the yield comes from: 500 mL of syrup weighs about 660 g, and losing that water leaves about 540 g of maple butter.

Grainy, Runny or Hard: How to Fix It

These fixes come from Cornell's guide, which is written for producers making it by the pot and holds just as well at home.

Grainy

It was stirred too warm, or you stopped stirring too soon and larger crystals grew. Next time cool it further and keep stirring until it is fully opaque.

Runny, or syrup on top

It did not boil quite high enough, so some syrup separates out in storage. Stir it back together; next batch, boil to the top of the range.

Set hard in the pan

It was stirred too long. Mist it with a little hot water and stir, or stand the pan in hot water until it softens, then jar it.

Crystals while cooling

A crust is forming on the surface. Mist it lightly with clean water, which dissolves the surface crystals, and keep it still.

Do not warm finished maple butter above 49°C (120°F) to soften it. Cornell warns that reheating makes it grainy and separate sooner.

Whipped Maple Butter: The Five-Minute Version

Many recipes called maple butter are this one: dairy butter whipped with maple syrup, for warm bread, cornbread or pancakes. It needs no thermometer. A dark syrup suits it best, because the maple has to come through a lot of butter.

Whipped Maple Butter

Prep5 min
CookNone
MakesAbout ⅔ cup
Keeps2 weeks, chilled
  • ½ cup (113 g)unsalted butter, softened
  • 2 tbsp (30 mL)pure maple syrup, Dark grade
  • ⅜ tspkosher salt
  • ¼ tspcinnamon, optional
  1. Beat the softened butter with a hand mixer or whisk for about 1 minute, until smooth and creamy.
  2. Add the maple syrup, salt and cinnamon. Beat for 1 to 2 minutes, until fluffy and lighter in color. The butter must be soft, or the syrup will not mix in and you get lumps.
  3. Serve soft, or pack into a small dish and chill. Bring it back to room temperature and re-whip if syrup beads on top.

The ratio and storage times follow Chef Lindsey Farr, who keeps it two weeks in the fridge or three months frozen. For a stronger maple taste, The Kitchn simmers ½ cup of syrup until it reduces by a quarter, cools it, then whips it into a stick of butter.

How to Store Maple Butter

Keep maple butter in the fridge. It has no preservatives, so Cornell's guide gives it less than a month at room temperature, where it can mold on the surface or separate. In a sealed glass jar in the fridge it keeps a month or two with little separation, and the guide puts its refrigerated shelf life at six months. For up to a year, freeze it.

A thin layer of syrup on top in storage is normal. Stir it back in. Whipped dairy maple butter is different: treat it like butter and use it within two weeks.

The same rules on mold and crystals that apply to the syrup itself are set out in our guide to whether maple syrup goes bad.

How to Use Maple Butter

Spread it on anything warm and bready. Cornell lists toast, bagels, muffins, pancakes and doughnuts, and it softens into a thin glaze on hot food.

  • Breakfast: on toast, a split English muffin, pancakes or waffles, in place of butter and syrup both.
  • Baking: swirled through the top of a banana bread or spread between the layers of a cake.
  • Dessert: a spoonful on vanilla ice cream, or on apple slices.
  • Vegetables: whipped maple butter melted over roasted squash or maple glazed carrots.

Key Takeaways

  • True maple butter is maple syrup only: boil 2 cups to 12 to 13°C above water's boiling point, 112 to 113°C at sea level.
  • Cool it in ice water, untouched, to 24°C (75°F) or lower before stirring.
  • Stir slowly until it turns pale, opaque and spreadable, 10 to 30 minutes.
  • Use a light syrup such as Golden. Keep it in the fridge; freeze it for longer.
  • Whipped maple butter is ½ cup soft butter beaten with 2 tablespoons of dark syrup and a pinch of salt.
