Sugar Maple vs Red Maple: How to Tell Them Apart, and Which to Tap

Sugar maple vs red maple comes down to the leaf notch, the bud color and the sap. A sugar maple has five smooth lobes with rounded gaps, tan buds and the sweetest sap of any maple. A red maple has toothed lobes with sharp gaps, red buds, and sap that needs about half again as much to make the same syrup.

By Maple Terroir 8 min read
Published October 19, 2026 Last updated October 19, 2026

The short answer: tap the sugar maple first, and tap the red maple too.

Sugar maple sap takes about 40 gallons to make one gallon of syrup, and red maple sap takes about 60, according to the University of New Hampshire Extension. Red maples also bud earlier in spring, which ends their season first. To tell them apart, look for a U-shaped gap between the leaf lobes on a sugar maple and a V-shaped gap on a red maple; in winter, look at the buds, tan on a sugar maple and bright red on a red maple.

Ten maple species are native to Canada, and sugar maple and red maple are easy to confuse. The Canadian Encyclopedia counts the ten, and these two share the same eastern forests, the same five-pointed silhouette from a distance and the same habit of turning red in the fall.

Up close they differ in almost every part: leaf, bud, bark, flower, seed, wood and sap. This guide goes through each one in the order you would check them on a walk, then covers which tree to tap if you want to make syrup in your own backyard.

How to Tell Them Apart by the Leaf

Look at the gap between the lobes. A sugar maple leaf has five lobes separated by rounded, U-shaped gaps, and a red maple leaf has sharp, V-shaped gaps, the one feature both the University of Guelph Arboretum and the University of New Hampshire Extension lead with. The Extension's memory aid: there is a U in sugar.

Count the lobes

A sugar maple leaf almost always has five lobes, delicately rounded at the base. A red maple leaf has three main lobes, and many leaves carry two small extra lobes near the stem, which is why field guides list it as three to five.

Run a finger along the edge

The edge of a sugar maple leaf is smooth between its few large points. A red maple leaf is toothed all the way around, like a fine saw, and you can feel it.

Ignore the color in summer

A red maple's leaves are green all summer like any other maple. The name comes from its buds, twigs and spring flowers, which are bright red, so leaf color only helps in the fall.

How to Tell Them Apart in Winter

Check the buds first, then the bark. Red maple buds are bright red and stand out against snow, while sugar maple buds are brown to tan and very sharp-pointed, with tight scales. This matters because sap runs in late winter and early spring, when the trees are bare and the leaf test is gone.

Buds and twigs

Red maple twigs are reddish and glossy, with small pale dots (lenticels, the breathing pores in the bark). Sugar maple twigs are brown, slender and shiny, and the bud at the tip of each twig comes to a sharp point.

Bark

A young red maple has smooth, light gray bark that can be mistaken for a beech. As it ages, the bark breaks into long, narrow plates that look shaggy and peel off easily. Sugar maple bark darkens and furrows with age, and even when it looks rough it stays tight to the trunk, often in a small diamond pattern.

The easiest fix is to mark your trees in the fall, while the leaves still tell you which is which. A strip of flagging tape on each red maple saves a guess in March.

Flowers and Seeds

Red maple flowers first and drops its seeds first. Its flowers are usually bright red and open in early spring, before the leaves, and its winged seeds ripen in late spring and early summer, according to the Virginia Tech dendrology fact sheet.

Sugar maple flowers are small, light yellow-green clusters on long stalks that open with the leaves or just before them. Its seeds, the paired "helicopters" children throw, are horseshoe-shaped, about an inch long, and turn brown when they ripen in the fall.

Fall Color

Red maples turn scarlet and sugar maples carry most of the orange. The University of Guelph Arboretum describes red maple leaves turning "a stunning shade of scarlet," and credits the sugar maple with most of the orange in an Ontario fall. Both can show red on the same hillside, so in October the leaf shape is still the better test.

Where Each Grows, and What the Wood Is For

Sugar maple is a forest tree of rich hardwood stands, and red maple grows almost anywhere. The Canadian Encyclopedia places sugar maple in hardwood forests from Ontario to the Maritimes, and red maple in "a variety of habitats from swamps to dry woods," which is why one of its old names is swamp maple.

Size

A sugar maple grows up to 35 m tall with a dense, rounded crown and is often the dominant tree in the canopy of northeastern hardwood forests. A red maple is a medium-sized tree, up to about 90 feet.

Hard maple and soft maple

Sugar maple wood is strong and fine-grained, valued for furniture, musical instruments and panelling. Red maple is sold as a soft maple: still strong and even, used for furniture, crates and flooring, but softer and more porous than sugar maple.

Sap and Syrup: Which Tree to Tap

Both trees make real maple syrup; the sugar maple makes it with less boiling. The University of Guelph Arboretum gives sugar maple sap the highest sugar content of Ontario's maples, about 3 to 6 percent, so 30 to 40 liters of sap make one liter of syrup. Red maple sap is less sweet, at about 60 gallons of sap per gallon of syrup by the University of New Hampshire Extension's figure.

Why the sugar reading matters

Finished syrup is about 66 percent sugar, so every point of sugar in the sap cuts the boiling. The Rule of 86 turns a sap reading into gallons: divide 86 by the sugar percentage. Our sap to syrup ratio calculator does it for any reading or species.

Pull the red maple taps first

Red maples bud earlier than sugar maples, and syrup boiled from sap collected after the buds open, called buddy syrup, tastes off. The University of New Hampshire Extension's advice: when the red maple buds start to swell, pull those taps and keep the sugar maples running.

How many taps each tree can take

The safe number of taps depends on the trunk's diameter, and the same rule applies to both species. Our maple tree tap calculator takes the diameter and species and gives the number, and the backyard tapping guide covers the tools, the timing and every maple worth tapping.

What About Norway Maple and Silver Maple?

Norway maple is the street tree most often taken for a sugar maple. Purdue Extension gives the quickest test: snap off a leaf stem and squeeze it, and a Norway maple bleeds milky sap where a sugar maple's is clear. Its leaves often carry five to seven lobes, and its paired seeds spread out almost flat. It came from Europe and, per the Canadian Encyclopedia, is becoming naturalized in parts of Canada; its sap is less sweet than a sugar maple's.

Silver maple leaves are deeply cut, with silvery undersides. Its sap runs heavily but carries even less sugar than red maple's, as low as one percent, and our sap calculator lists it beside the other three.

Which Tree Fits What You Want

  • The most syrup per hour of boiling: sugar maple.
  • A tree for a damp corner of the yard: red maple, which grows from swamps to dry woods.
  • Orange fall color: sugar maple. Scarlet: red maple.
  • Hardwood for furniture or flooring: sugar maple, the hard maple.
