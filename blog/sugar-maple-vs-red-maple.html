Sugar Maple vs Red Maple: How to Tell Them Apart, and Which to Tap

Both make maple syrup. A sugar maple gives sweeter sap and a longer season, a red maple gives less sugar and stops earlier. Here is how to tell them apart in summer and in the middle of tapping season, when the leaves are gone.

By Maple Terroir October 19, 2026 5 min read

Tap the sugar maple first.

Sugar maple sap takes about 40 gallons to make a gallon of syrup. Red maple sap takes about 60, according to the University of New Hampshire Extension, and red maples bud earlier in spring, which ends their season sooner. If you have both, tap both, and pull the red maple taps first.

In Summer, Look at a Leaf

Count the lobes, then run a finger along the edge. A sugar maple leaf has five lobes and smooth edges, and the gaps between the lobes are rounded. The University of New Hampshire Extension's memory aid: there is a U in sugar, and the gaps are U-shaped. A red maple leaf usually has three main lobes, a saw-toothed edge and sharp, V-shaped gaps.

Do not go by color. The Extension points out that a red maple's name comes from its buds, and its leaves are green all summer like any other maple.

In Tapping Season, Look at the Buds and Bark

Sap runs from late winter into early spring, when the trees are bare, so the leaf test is no help. Two things still are.

The buds. Red maple buds are bright red and easy to see against snow in late winter. Sugar maple buds are tan.

The bark. A young, fast-growing red maple has smooth gray bark. An older one breaks into narrow vertical plates that look shaggy and peel off easily. Sugar maple bark stays tight to the trunk, even when it looks rough, with a small diamond pattern.

Mark your trees with flagging tape in the fall, while the leaves still tell you which is which, and you will not need to guess in March.

Which One to Tap

Both. Sugar maple is the standard tree for syrup because its sap carries about two percent sugar or more, so less of it boils down to a gallon. Red maple sap is less sweet, so you boil more of it for the same syrup, but it makes real maple syrup all the same.

The catch with red maple is timing. Red maples bud earlier than sugar maples, and syrup boiled from sap collected after the buds open, called buddy syrup, tastes bad. The University of New Hampshire Extension's advice: when the red maple buds start to swell, pull those taps and keep the sugar maples running.

What About the Norway Maple?

The Norway maple is the street tree most often mistaken for a sugar maple, because both have five-pointed leaves. Purdue Extension gives a quick test: snap off a leaf stem and squeeze it. A Norway maple bleeds a milky sap; a sugar maple's sap is clear. Norway maple leaves often have five to seven lobes, and its paired seeds spread out almost flat, where a sugar maple's point downward.

A Norway maple can be tapped, but its sap is less sweet than a sugar maple's. It is also an invasive species in parts of North America, brought over from Europe.
