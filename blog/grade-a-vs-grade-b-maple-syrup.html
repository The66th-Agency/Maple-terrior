Maple Syrup Grades Explained: Grade A vs Grade B and What Changed

Canada has two maple syrup grades, Grade A and Processing Grade, and Grade A comes in four color classes set by how much light passes through the syrup. Here is every grade, how it is measured, what the old Canada No. 1 to No. 3 and American Grade B names meant, and which grade to buy for what you are cooking.

By Maple Terroir 14 min read
Published May 19, 2026 Last updated November 6, 2026

There is no Grade B maple syrup in Canada, and there never was. Canada's old grades were numbered: Canada No. 1, No. 2 and No. 3. In December 2014, Canada replaced them with Canada Grade A, sorted into four color classes, plus a separate Canada Processing Grade, and producers had until December 2016 to switch (Canada Gazette, SOR/2014-297). "Grade B" was an American grade: until March 2015 the U.S. standard filed the darkest, strongest syrup as "Grade B for Reprocessing", which is why the name still turns up in old recipes (Federal Register, January 29, 2015).

Retail maple syrup in Canada is now sold as Canada Grade A, in four color classes (Golden Delicate, Amber Rich, Dark Robust and Very Dark Strong). Every class has to pass the same Grade A tests, so the classes differ in color and strength of flavor, and the label names both.

The reason this matters: shoppers still see "Grade B" in old recipes and assume it is a separate, lower-quality product. The short answer is that the color tracks the season. Syrup from sap harvested at the start of the season is pale and mild, and syrup from late-season sap is darker and stronger (Maple from Canada). The flavor changes. The grade requirements do not.

The Old System (Before December 2014)

Canada's old system, under the Maple Products Regulations, graded syrup as Canada No. 1, No. 2 or No. 3, with color classes beneath them: Extra Light, Light and Medium under No. 1, Amber under No. 2 and Dark under No. 3. A number reads like a ranking, so a darker syrup labeled No. 2 looked second-rate on the shelf.

The industry told the regulator that this misled shoppers. In the regulation's summary, the International Maple Syrup Institute "believes that this misleads consumers into thinking that these syrups are of inferior quality to the lighter coloured ones graded as Canada No. 1, when such is not the case." The old grades also did not line up with the grades used by two provinces, Ontario among them, or with the United States grades, which made the same syrup carry different names depending on where it was sold.

Here are the old and new systems side by side, as that regulation records them (Canada Gazette, SOR/2014-297):

The 2014 Change and What It Created

Canada's new system, in force since December 2014, puts retail syrup under Canada Grade A, in four color classes named for color and taste. The International Maple Syrup Institute, which represents producers, associations and governments in Canada and the United States, asked for the change on both sides of the border. The regulation states its purpose as "to address potential consumer confusion with regard to the grading of dark and stronger tasting maple syrups" (Canada Gazette, SOR/2014-297). The USDA adopted the same four classes and the same light cut-offs on March 2, 2015 (Federal Register, January 29, 2015), so a Golden or a Very Dark label means the same thing in both countries.

01

Grade A Golden Color, Delicate Taste

The first sap of the season produces Golden Delicate. Light gold in color, with a soft buttery character and a subtle sweetness that does not push past the food it sits on. Pour it on a wedge of aged cheddar, drizzle it over vanilla ice cream, or finish a bowl of plain Greek yogurt with it. The lighter the dish, the better Golden performs. Cooks reach for it when they want maple in the background, not the foreground.

02

Grade A Amber Color, Rich Taste

The mid-season harvest produces Amber Rich. Color sits in the classic amber range that most people picture when they hear "maple syrup." The flavor is balanced, distinctly maple, neither delicate nor aggressive. Pancakes, waffles, French toast, oatmeal, yogurt, and most dessert applications run on Amber. If a family is keeping one bottle in the cupboard, Amber Rich is the right one.

03

Grade A Dark Color, Robust Taste

Late mid-season sap produces Dark Robust. The color deepens noticeably, and the maple character pushes forward. Bakers reach for Dark when they want the maple flavor to survive the oven (cookies, granola, banana bread, maple-glazed nuts). It also handles coffee and lattes better than the lighter grades because the flavor does not get washed out by the bitterness of the espresso.

04

Grade A Very Dark Color, Strong Taste

Late-season sap produces Very Dark Strong Taste. Under the U.S. standard before 2015, syrup this dark was filed as Grade B for Reprocessing. Color is deep mahogany, the maple character is intense, and the flavor stands up to almost anything. It suits meat glazes, marinades, BBQ sauces, and any reduction where maple needs to register against pepper, soy, vinegar, or smoke. If a recipe written before 2015 calls for Grade B, this is what it means.

How a Maple Syrup Grade Is Measured

Every grade comes down to two measurements: how much sugar the syrup holds, and how much light passes through it. The rules are in the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's grade compendium, which the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations incorporate by reference (Canadian Grade Compendium, Volume 7). Taste counts too: a Grade A syrup has to have the maple flavor its color class calls for.

First, the sugar. Every grade has to hold 66 to 68.9 percent soluble solids, read with a refractometer or a hydrometer at 20 degrees Celsius. The 68.9 percent ceiling arrived with the 2014 rules, which added it to help keep syrup from crystallizing (Canada Gazette, SOR/2014-297). Because every grade sits in the same window of under 3 points, a Golden and a Very Dark syrup hold close to the same amount of sugar.

Then the color class, the part the label shows. A spectrophotometer shines light at a wavelength of 560 nanometers through a square glass cell with a 10 mm light path, and reads the percent that comes out the other side, against analytical-grade glycerol set at 100 percent. A visual glass comparator built to the same optics may be used instead. Pick a class below to see the test.

The French names on a Canadian label, Doré, Ambré, Foncé and Très Foncé, follow the same four cut-offs. The darker the class, the stronger the taste, and the later in the season the syrup tends to come from: Maple from Canada describes early syrup as clear with a slightly sweet taste, and late-season syrup as darker and more caramelized.

The five tests for Canada Grade A

From section 3 of the compendium. Every Grade A bottle, Golden to Very Dark, passes all five.

  1. 66% to 68.9% soluble solids, read at 20 °C
  2. Free from fermentation
  3. Uniform in color, with no sediment, cloudiness or turbidity
  4. A color class, set by the light test above
  5. A maple flavor true to its color class, with no objectionable odor or taste

Canada Processing Grade

From section 4. The same sugar window as Grade A, for syrup that does not meet tests 3, 4 or 5. It has no color class.

  1. 66% to 68.9% soluble solids
  2. Fails at least one of tests 3 to 5, such as an off-flavor or a cloudy syrup

Canada Processing Grade, the Second Grade

Canada has two maple syrup grades, not one. Grade A is the grade you find in stores. The other is Canada Processing Grade, which the 2014 regulation created for "off-flavoured, and otherwise defective syrups," allowing "other uses for processing grade such as further food processing or other non-food uses" (Canada Gazette, SOR/2014-297).

Processing Grade is still maple syrup, with the same sugar content. It is the grade for syrup that fails tests 3 to 5 above: its color is uneven or it carries sediment or cloudiness, it cannot be given a color class, or its flavor is off. It carries no color class, so a bottle that names Golden, Amber, Dark or Very Dark on its label is Grade A.

The regulator does not stop Processing Grade being sold in stores, but the grade name has to be on the label, so a shopper can always tell the two apart.

This is the part of the system that answers the old Grade B worry. The darkest syrup in Canada is Grade A Very Dark, graded on the same tests as Golden. Darkness is a color class. Defects are a different grade.

Why the Change Mattered

The new names describe color and taste, so the label tells a shopper what the syrup tastes like instead of implying a ranking.

The change did three things the old numbers could not. It put every retail syrup, light or dark, under one grade with the same five tests, so a darker bottle no longer carries a lower number. It moved defective syrup into a grade of its own, so a defect is named on the label instead of hiding inside a color. And it gave Canada and the United States the same four classes and the same light cut-offs, so a bottle of Very Dark means the same thing on both sides of the border.

For a cook, the practical result is a label that answers the real question. "Golden Color, Delicate Taste" says what is in the bottle and what it will do to the dish.

Which Grade Should I Use?

Pancakes, French Toast, Yogurt

Use Case

Amber Rich. The most versatile grade for breakfast and table use. Balanced enough that it does not overwhelm, distinctly maple enough that nobody mistakes it for sugar water. Default choice for a family kitchen. We do not sell Amber, so for pancakes pick our Golden for a lighter pour or our Dark for a stronger one.

Coffee, Lattes, Baking, Glazes

Use Case

Dark Robust or Very Dark Strong. The maple character needs to push through espresso, butter, brown sugar, or a glaze that includes vinegar, mustard, or soy. Lighter grades wash out. Use Case

Golden Delicate. The job here is to add a thin layer of maple without overpowering an aged cheddar, a scoop of vanilla, or a bowl of fresh berries. Golden carries enough sweetness to register without crowding the base ingredient.

Marinades, Meat Reductions, BBQ

Use Case

Very Dark Strong Taste. Roughly the old American Grade B. Built for situations where maple needs to register against salt, pepper, smoke, and acid.

All-Purpose Family Kitchen

Use Case

Amber Rich. If keeping one bottle, this is it, because it leans neither light nor strong. We do not sell Amber. Our Golden and Dark bottles cover the lighter and stronger ends.

What People Still Call Grade B

Old recipe cards, cookbooks and forum threads still say "Grade B", because for decades it was the American name for the darkest, strongest syrup. Before March 2015 the U.S. standard called it "Grade B for Reprocessing" and did not intend it for retail sale, even though, as the USDA itself noted, shoppers were "increasingly seeking the darkest color class of maple syrup for cooking and table use" (Federal Register, January 29, 2015).

The 2015 change moved that syrup, when it has no damage or off-flavors, into Grade A so it could be sold in retail bottles. In both countries today, the nearest match to an old Grade B is Grade A Very Dark, Strong Taste, and a recipe that asks for Grade B will work with Very Dark or, for a slightly milder result, Dark.

So recognize the old term when you see it and do not chase it as a separate product. The decision the cook is making is how strong a maple taste the dish needs, and the four color classes answer that directly.

Three Misconceptions About Maple Syrup Grades

"Grade A is better than Grade B"

Not today. Under the old U.S. standard, Grade B named the darkest syrup and its intended use, reprocessing, which is why it read as second-rate. Since 2015 that syrup, free of off-flavors, is Grade A Very Dark and passes the same Grade A tests as Golden. A letter on an old recipe card tells you the color, not the quality.

"Darker maple syrup is lower quality"

Wrong. Every Grade A class has to be free from fermentation, sediment and cloudiness and carry a maple flavor true to its color, so a Very Dark bottle meets the same requirements as a Golden one. A dark syrup that fails those tests is not a darker class; it is Processing Grade, and the label has to say so.

"All Grade A tastes the same"

Wrong. Grade A is one set of quality tests, not one flavor. Its four classes run from Golden, which Maple from Canada calls mildly sweet, to Very Dark, its most pronounced taste. Taste a Golden and a Very Dark side by side and the difference is obvious, which is why the class and its taste are printed on the label.
