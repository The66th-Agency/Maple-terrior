Grade A vs Grade B Maple Syrup: What Changed and Which to Buy

Canada never had a Grade B. Here is what the old grades were, what replaced them in 2014, and how to pick the right grade for what you are cooking.

By Maple Terroir May 19, 2026 10 min read

There is no Grade B maple syrup in Canada, and there never was. Canada's old grades were numbered: Canada No. 1, No. 2 and No. 3. In December 2014, Canada replaced them with Canada Grade A, sorted into four color classes, plus a separate Canada Processing Grade, and producers had until December 2016 to switch (Canada Gazette, SOR/2014-297). "Grade B" was the old American name for very dark syrup, which is why it still turns up in recipes.

Retail maple syrup in Canada is now sold as Canada Grade A, in four color classes (Golden Delicate, Amber Rich, Dark Robust and Very Dark Strong) that differ by flavor intensity, not by quality. The old idea that a darker, lower-ranked syrup was inferior was always wrong, and the new names were meant to fix it.

The reason this matters in 2026: shoppers see "Grade B" in old recipes and on small-batch labels and assume it is a separate, lower-quality product. The truth is shorter and more useful. The grade reflects when the sap was harvested that season. Early sap makes light syrup. Late sap makes dark syrup. The flavor changes. The quality does not.

The Old System (Before December 2014)

Canada's old system graded retail syrup as Canada No. 1, No. 2 or No. 3, with color classes from Extra Light to Dark. A number or a letter reads like a quality ranking, so shoppers took the darker, lower-numbered syrup as second-rate.

Producers and chefs knew this was wrong. The darker syrup was simply stronger in flavor, harvested at the end of the sugaring season when the sap carried a deeper maple character. Bakers and restaurants preferred it for cooking, glazing and baking because the flavor stood up to other ingredients.

The mismatch between perceived and actual quality was the headline issue: the darker syrup was equal in quality, only different in flavor intensity, and the naming made it look inferior. The industry needed a system that communicated what was actually different across the grades, which is flavor, not quality.

The 2014 Change and What It Created

Canada's new system, in force since December 2014, puts all retail syrup under Canada Grade A, in four color classes named for color and taste. The International Maple Syrup Institute, made up of maple associations in Canada and the United States, proposed the change.

"Light through it" is how the grades are measured: the percent of light that passes through a sample of the syrup. The cut-offs are identical in the USDA standard and the Canadian Food Inspection Agency grade compendium.

01

Grade A Golden Color, Delicate Taste

The first sap of the season produces Golden Delicate. Light gold in color, with a soft buttery character and a subtle sweetness that does not push past the food it sits on. Pour it on a wedge of aged cheddar, drizzle it over vanilla ice cream, or finish a bowl of plain Greek yogurt with it. The lighter the dish, the better Golden performs. Cooks reach for it when they want maple in the background, not the foreground.

02

Grade A Amber Color, Rich Taste

The mid-season harvest produces Amber Rich. Color sits in the classic amber range that most people picture when they hear "maple syrup." The flavor is balanced, distinctly maple, neither delicate nor aggressive. Pancakes, waffles, French toast, oatmeal, yogurt, and most dessert applications run on Amber. If a family is keeping one bottle in the cupboard, Amber Rich is the right one.

03

Grade A Dark Color, Robust Taste

Late mid-season sap produces Dark Robust. The color deepens noticeably, and the maple character pushes forward. Bakers reach for Dark when they want the maple flavor to survive the oven (cookies, granola, banana bread, maple-glazed nuts). It also handles coffee and lattes better than the lighter grades because the flavor does not get washed out by the bitterness of the espresso.

04

Grade A Very Dark Color, Strong Taste

The final harvest of the season produces Very Dark Strong Taste. In the old American system, this was roughly the syrup called Grade B. Color is deep mahogany, the maple character is intense, and the flavor stands up to almost anything. Professional kitchens use Very Dark for meat glazes, marinades, BBQ sauces, and any reduction where maple needs to register against pepper, soy, vinegar, or smoke. If a recipe written before 2015 calls for Grade B, this is what it means.

Why the Change Mattered

The new names describe color and taste, so the label tells a shopper what the syrup tastes like instead of implying a quality ranking.

The naming change forced the conversation onto flavor intensity, which is the actual difference between the four grades. Golden Delicate and Very Dark Strong are not better or worse than each other. They are different tools for different jobs. A regulator ranking them on a single quality scale was misleading buyers and limiting how producers could sell.

The new system also made the four grades more legible to anyone reading a label for the first time. "Golden Color, Delicate Taste" tells a shopper what is in the bottle.

Which Grade Should I Use?

Pancakes, French Toast, Yogurt

Use Case

Amber Rich. The most versatile grade for breakfast and table use. Balanced enough that it does not overwhelm, distinctly maple enough that nobody mistakes it for sugar water. Default choice for a family kitchen. We do not sell Amber, so for pancakes pick our Golden for a lighter pour or our Dark for a stronger one.

Coffee, Lattes, Baking, Glazes

Use Case

Dark Robust or Very Dark Strong. The maple character needs to push through espresso, butter, brown sugar, or a glaze that includes vinegar, mustard, or soy. Lighter grades wash out. Dark and Very Dark survive. When you swap syrup in for the sugar itself, our sugar to maple syrup conversion calculator gives the exact amounts.

Finishing Cheese, Ice Cream, Fresh Fruit

Use Case

Golden Delicate. The job here is to add a thin layer of maple without overpowering an aged cheddar, a scoop of vanilla, or a bowl of fresh berries. Golden carries enough sweetness to register without crowding the base ingredient.

Marinades, Meat Reductions, BBQ

Use Case

Very Dark Strong Taste. Roughly the old American Grade B. Built for situations where maple needs to register against salt, pepper, smoke, and acid.

All-Purpose Family Kitchen

Use Case

Amber Rich. If keeping one bottle, this is it, because it leans neither light nor strong. We do not sell Amber. Our Golden and Dark bottles cover the lighter and stronger ends.

What People Still Call Grade B

Old recipe cards and some American producers still use the term "Grade B".

If a producer is selling something labeled "Grade B" in 2026, they almost certainly mean Grade A Very Dark, Strong Taste under the new system. Same product, same flavor profile, old name. The legal grade on the bottle (the one the regulator audits) will be Grade A Very Dark. The handwritten chalkboard at the farm stand might say Grade B. Both are pointing at the same bottle of syrup.

The takeaway for shoppers is to recognize the old term when they see it and not chase it as a separate product. Anyone hunting Grade B online or at a market is looking for Very Dark Strong Taste. The new name communicates flavor intensity, which is the actual decision the cook is making.

Three Misconceptions About Maple Syrup Grades

"Grade A is better than Grade B"

Wrong. Grade B was equal in quality to Grade A under the old American system, only darker and stronger in flavor. The letter B implied a quality ranking that never existed in the production reality. The American change in 2015 removed the letter precisely because it kept misleading shoppers on the very point being asked.

"Darker maple syrup is lower quality"

Wrong. Darker maple syrup carries stronger flavor because the sap was harvested later in the season, when sugar content shifts and natural compounds in the sap concentrate. Quality depends on the producer and how the syrup is made, not on the color.

"All Grade A tastes the same"

Wrong. The four Grade A sub-grades cover a real flavor spectrum. Anyone who tastes Golden Delicate and Very Dark Strong side by side will register the difference immediately. The "all Grade A" framing is what made the new system necessary in the first place. Grade A is a regulatory umbrella, not a flavor profile.
