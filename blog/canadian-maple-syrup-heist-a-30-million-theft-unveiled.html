The Great Canadian Maple Syrup Heist: 9,571 Barrels Gone

By Kai Lytton December 21, 2024 6 min read

Quick answer

The Great Canadian Maple Syrup Heist was one of the largest agricultural thefts in Canadian history. Across 2011 and 2012, thieves siphoned syrup from Quebec's strategic reserve and resold it, until an inventory check exposed the scheme and led to a string of arrests and convictions.

Updated October 2, 2026

The Great Canadian Maple Syrup Heist is one of the most unusual crimes in Canadian history. Over several months in 2011 and 2012, thieves took 9,571 barrels of maple syrup, valued at $18.7 million, from a warehouse of Quebec's strategic reserve.

For the longer history of maple syrup in Canada, read The Rich Story of Maple Syrup Canada.

The thieves targeted Quebec's Strategic Maple Syrup Reserve, a facility that safeguarded millions of pounds of pure maple syrup. This meticulously planned operation resulted in the disappearance of nearly 3,000 tons of maple syrup. Authorities launched a complex investigation that crossed borders between two countries and exposed an underground network of sticky-fingered thieves.

That reserve exists because Canadian maple syrup production is one of the most tightly regulated agricultural industries in the world, something the 2025 production report puts into sharp perspective.

Quebec produces 72 percent of the world's maple syrup, according to Maple from Canada, and dominates the world's maple syrup market. Their Federation of Quebec Maple Syrup Producers (QMSP) runs what people call the International Strategic Reserve (ISR). Think of it as OPEC, but they deal with maple syrup instead of oil.

Anyone who wants the real product behind that production system can browse our Canadian maple syrup catalog, drawn straight from the Appalachian Quebec sugar bush.

This reserve plays a vital role in stabilizing global supply and prices. They store extra syrup during bumper harvests and release it during peak seasons. This stockpile became so valuable that it attracted one of Canada's most infamous heists.

Anatomy of the Heist

The syrup was kept in a warehouse in Saint-Louis-de-Blandford owned by the spouse of Avik Caron, who became the insider in the scheme. Over several months in 2011 and 2012, thieves removed 9,571 barrels of syrup, valued at $18.7 million.

These criminals executed their plan with precision. The sticky operation unfolded this way:

  • Rented space in the same industrial facility to run a legitimate business
  • Transported barrels to remote sugar shacks to siphon syrup
  • Refilled original barrels with water to avoid detection
  • Started direct siphoning at the reserve
  • Used tanker trucks to move the syrup

Richard Vallières emerged as the mastermind. His decade-long black market experience proved valuable. He led a small team that took advantage of the annual barrel inspection schedule.

The thieves trucked the syrup east to New Brunswick and south to Vermont and sold it to buyers who did not know it was stolen.

The operation reached staggering proportions. The facility lost approximately 2,700 tons of maple syrup before anyone noticed.

Investigation Breakthrough

The theft came to light in July 2012, during the annual inventory at the Saint-Louis-de-Blandford warehouse, when inspectors found barrels that were suspiciously light and held water instead of maple syrup.

Evidence Trail

The Sûreté du Québec launched its largest investigation to date. The team conducted over 300 interviews and executed 40 search warrants.

International Cooperation

The case grew beyond Canadian borders and needed unprecedented teamwork between:

  • The Royal Canadian Mounted Police
  • Quebec Provincial Police
  • U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement

Investigators uncovered a distribution network spanning New Brunswick, Ontario, Vermont, and New Hampshire. In December 2012, police arrested 17 men, including Richard Vallières, the alleged mastermind, who later admitted he had been active in the black market for a decade before the heist.

Legal Aftermath

The Supreme Court of Canada delivered its final verdict in 2022, which ended a decade-long legal battle.

Criminal Proceedings

Richard Vallières was sentenced in April 2017 to eight years in prison and a $9.4 million fine, the heaviest penalty in the case.

Recovery Efforts

Police later recovered hundreds of barrels of the stolen syrup from an exporter in Kedgwick, New Brunswick.

Financial Penalties

After the Quebec Court of Appeal reduced the fine, the Supreme Court of Canada reinstated the $9.4 million fine in a unanimous 2022 decision, holding that the fine reflects the value of the syrup that passed through his hands, not just his personal profit.

The court set a vital precedent that crime profits alone cannot determine penalty amounts. Vallières received ten years to pay the fine and faces an additional six-year prison term if he fails to comply.

Security at the Reserve Since the Heist

The heist exposed weak security. The Saint-Louis-de-Blandford facility had minimal security arrangements, without cameras or alarm systems, and syrup was stored in unmarked white metal barrels inspected only once a year (Wikipedia). The only security was a guard who was supposed to stop by each day (Brendan Borrell).

The federation has since added surveillance and monitoring across its warehouses (City Towner, 2025). The Laurierville facility, known as la forteresse du sirop d'érable (the maple syrup fortress), has camera coverage and access systems that need special codes. More frequent barrel inspections replaced the annual schedule that let the thieves operate undetected for nearly a year.

FAQs

Q1. How much maple syrup was stolen in the Canadian heist? About 2,700 tonnes of maple syrup, 9,571 barrels, were stolen from a storage facility in Quebec over several months in 2011 and 2012. Early reports estimated the value at around $30 million, but the stolen syrup was later valued at $18.7 million.

Q2. Who was the mastermind behind the maple syrup theft? Richard Vallières was identified as the ringleader of the operation. He had a reputation in the maple syrup industry as a "barrel roller" who found ways to circumvent the regulated supply management system.

Q3. How did the thieves manage to steal such a large quantity of maple syrup? The thieves rented space in the same industrial facility, siphoned syrup from barrels in remote locations, refilled the original barrels with water to avoid detection, and eventually began direct siphoning at the reserve using tanker trucks for transportation.

Q4. What were the legal consequences for those involved in the heist? The mastermind, Richard Vallières, was sentenced in April 2017 to eight years in prison and a $9.4 million fine. In 2022 the Supreme Court of Canada reinstated that fine after the Quebec Court of Appeal had reduced it. In total, 17 men were arrested in connection with the theft.

Q5. How did this heist impact the maple syrup industry in Canada? The theft led to changes in security at Quebec's strategic reserve, including more frequent barrel inspections, cameras and coded access at the Laurierville warehouse.
