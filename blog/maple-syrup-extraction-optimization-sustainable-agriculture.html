How Maple Producers Get More Syrup From Each Tree

By Maple Terroir March 4, 2025 5 min read

The USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service reports that maple syrup production in the United States reached 5.1 million gallons in 2022.

For what the process looks like season to season, read From Sap to Sweetness.

Maple producers have moved from collecting sap in buckets to tubing, vacuum and reverse osmosis systems. This guide covers the equipment producers use, how they look after the sugar bush, how they time the season and how they adapt to a changing climate.

Equipment That Gets More From Each Tree

Producers rely on three kinds of equipment to collect and boil sap more efficiently.

Reverse Osmosis

Reverse osmosis (RO) removes much of the water from sap before it is boiled. Producers who use an RO system cut their evaporator fuel use by 50 to 80 percent, and University of Wisconsin Extension reports that RO can reduce energy needs by 70 percent compared with boiling in an open pan.

Vacuum Tubing

Vacuum tubing can increase sap production by 50 percent to more than 200 percent over a season, according to the New York State Maple Tubing and Vacuum System Notebook, because the vacuum keeps sap moving from the tree into the tubing.

Evaporator Preheaters

A properly sized preheater raises evaporator efficiency by about 15 to 20 percent [5][14].

Looking After the Sugar Bush

A sugar bush keeps producing only as long as the forest stays healthy.

Checking Tree Health

Healthy maple trees can be tapped for many decades and even centuries. Producers check their trees in winter, when bare canopies make ice and wind damage easy to see [7]. Light, creamy wood shavings show a healthy tap hole. Dark shavings mean the drill hit old, dead wood.

Tapping Guidelines

  • Measure the trunk 4.5 feet above the ground.

  • Do not tap a trunk under 10 inches across.

  • Put one tap in a trunk of 10 to 17 inches.

  • Put two taps in a trunk of 18 inches or more, never more than two.

Keeping the Forest Diverse

Sugar bushes also provide wildlife habitat, recreation, clean air, soil retention and water regulation [6]. Producers keep other tree species growing among the maples, because a mixed forest is less exposed to pest outbreaks [9][10]. Thinning the stand reduces moisture stress during droughts [9].

Timing the Season

Sap runs best when temperatures drop below freezing at night and rise into the 40s (°F) during the day. Those swings usually come in March, though the season can run from mid-February through April [12].

Clean Equipment and Cold Sap

Producers clean collection and storage tanks between sap runs, and they place storage tanks on the north or northwest side of the sugarhouse, out of direct sun and with ventilation [13]. They keep stored sap below 38°F and boil it within seven days so microbes do not spoil it [16].

Adapting to a Changing Climate

Climate change is shifting the maple season, and the Maple Climate Network studies how sugar bushes can adapt [17]. Producers and researchers track sap flow under vacuum, soil moisture and temperature, tree pressure and temperature, the weather, and the daily sugar content of the sap [17].

Researchers project that the tapping season could start up to one month earlier by 2100 than it did from 1950 to 2017 [18], and that sap collection starts about 4.3 days earlier for each 1°C rise in March temperature [19].

Producers adapt by mixing tree species, including red maples, and by using vacuum tubing to collect sap in uneven seasons [11]. A diverse forest also stands up better to climate stress [9].

Conclusion

Producers get more syrup from each tree by pairing modern equipment with careful forest care: they tap only trees large enough, limit the number of taps, keep the sugar bush diverse and healthy, and time the season to the weather.
