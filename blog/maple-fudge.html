Maple Fudge Made With Pure Maple Syrup

Old-fashioned maple fudge from four ingredients: maple syrup, whipping cream, butter and walnuts. Boil to 114°C, cool to 45°C, beat until it turns dull and creamy, and let it set. No corn syrup, no sweetened condensed milk.

Maple fudge is maple syrup and cream boiled to 114 to 115°C (237 to 239°F), cooled without stirring, then beaten until tiny sugar crystals form and turn it thick, pale and creamy. The maple syrup is the only sweetener, so the fudge tastes of maple first and caramel second.

This is the old-fashioned method, and it rewards a thermometer and a little patience. The recipe card below makes 36 squares in a 20 cm (8 inch) pan from two 250 mL bottles of syrup. After it you will find a picture of each stage, why the temperatures matter, how to fix grainy or soft fudge, which syrup to use, how to top it with maple sugar and how long it keeps.

Maple Walnut Fudge

Prep10 min
Cook20 to 60 min
Cool and beat35 min
Set2 to 3 hours

Ingredients

  • 2 cups (500 mL)pure maple syrup, two 250 mL bottles
  • 1 cup (250 mL)whipping cream, 35%
  • 3 tbsp (45 g)butter, plus a little for the pan
  • ½ cup (60 g)walnuts, chopped
  • 1 tbspmaple sugar, for the top, optional

Makes 36 squares of about 3 cm (1¼ inch).

Method

  1. Line a 20 cm (8 inch) square pan with parchment paper, leaving it hanging over two sides to lift the fudge out later.
  2. Put the maple syrup and butter in a heavy pot of at least 4 L (4 quarts). Bring to a boil over medium-high heat, then turn it down and simmer for 5 minutes.
  3. Pour in the cream. It will foam up. Clip on a candy thermometer and cook without stirring until it reads 114 to 115°C (237 to 239°F). This takes 20 minutes to an hour, depending on your pot and burner.
  4. Take the pot off the heat straight away. Do not stir. Set it in a sink of cold water and let it cool, untouched, to 43 to 50°C (110 to 122°F), about 20 to 30 minutes.
  5. Beat with an electric mixer for 2 to 5 minutes, until the fudge thickens, turns paler and loses its gloss.
  6. Stir in the walnuts quickly and pour into the pan. Do not scrape the sides of the pot. Smooth the top and scatter the maple sugar over it.
  7. Refrigerate for 2 to 3 hours, until firm. Lift it out by the paper and cut 6 by 6 into 36 squares.

Tools

  • Heavy pot, 4 L or larger
  • Candy thermometer
  • Electric hand mixer
  • 20 cm (8 inch) square pan
  • Parchment paper

The Four Stages of Maple Fudge

Fudge is a question of controlling when the sugar crystallizes. You concentrate the syrup, cool it while it stays smooth, then make it crystallize all at once by beating, so the crystals stay too small to feel. Each stage has a number to watch.

114°C237°F

1. Boil

Cream and syrup boil, untouched, until enough water has gone for the fudge to set firm enough to cut.

45°C113°F

2. Cool

Off the heat, in cold water, no stirring. The syrup holds more sugar than it can keep dissolved, and waits.

2 to 5min

3. Beat

Beating starts millions of small crystals at once. Stop when it thickens and the shine goes matte.

2 to 3hours

4. Set

In the pan, in the fridge, until firm enough to cut clean squares.

Why the Temperatures Matter

The cooking temperature sets how firm the fudge is. Maple from Canada's maple fudge recipe cooks to 114 to 115°C (237 to 239°F) and calls the thermometer the secret to success. Ricardo's maple fudge goes to 240°F (115°C). Below 114°C there is too much water left and the fudge stays soft; much above 115°C it sets hard and crumbly.

The cooling temperature sets how smooth it is. Both recipes cool the fudge to about 43°C (110°F) before beating. Cornell's maple program explains why in its confections notebook: stirring hot fudge starts crystals too early and gives it a gritty texture, while the cooler the syrup is when you start, the smaller the crystals stay.

Maple syrup goes through the same stages on its own, without cream. The chart shows where fudge sits beside the pure maple confections, using Cornell's finish temperatures.

Check your thermometer first

A thermometer that reads two degrees off moves you from fudge to soup. Put it in a pot of boiling water: at sea level it should read 100°C (212°F). If it reads 98°C, because of altitude or the thermometer itself, take the fudge off at 112 to 113°C instead. Clip it so the tip sits in the syrup and does not touch the bottom of the pot.

Can You Make Maple Fudge Without a Thermometer?

You can, using the soft-ball test, though it takes practice. As the bubbles turn small and slow, drop half a teaspoon of the mixture into a glass of very cold water. At the soft-ball stage it gathers into a soft, sticky ball you can pick up, which flattens when you take it out of the water. If it dissolves into threads, keep cooking and test again every minute or two.

A candy thermometer takes the guessing out of the one step that decides whether the fudge sets, which is why both recipes we checked call for one.

Why Maple Fudge Turns Out Grainy, Soft or Hard

Every fudge problem traces back to one of three numbers: how hot it boiled, how cool it was before beating, and how long it was beaten.

Grainy or sandy

It was stirred while hot, beaten before it cooled, or the sugary crust from the sides of the pot went in. The maple producers' recipe warns that the layer on the sides is more granular than the rest, so pour and leave it behind.

Soft and will not set

It stopped short of 114°C, or it was not beaten long enough. Scrape it back into the pot with a few tablespoons of cream, boil it back to temperature, and cool and beat it again.

Hard and crumbly

It went past 115°C, or it was beaten too long and set in the bowl. Next time, take it off the heat on the dot and stop beating as soon as the shine goes.

Boiled over

The pot was too small. Syrup and cream foam up far above where they start, which is why the recipe calls for a pot of 4 L or more.

Which Maple Syrup to Use for Fudge

Use a dark syrup. Maple from Canada recommends dark syrup for this recipe for its robust flavor, and it holds its own against a cup of cream. The same goes for most baking, as our guide to the best maple syrup for baking explains. Use pure maple syrup only: table syrup is mostly corn syrup and will not set or taste the same.

A Maple Sugar Top

Maple sugar is maple syrup boiled until almost no water is left and stirred into dry granules, the last stage on the chart above. Scattered over the fudge right after you pour it, it adds a light crunch and a second hit of maple, with nothing that is not maple.

1

Pour and smooth

Spread the beaten fudge into the lined pan with a spatula while it is still soft.

2

Scatter at once

Sprinkle 1 tablespoon of maple sugar evenly over the top before the surface sets, so it sticks.

3

Press lightly

Lay a sheet of parchment on top and press with your palm, so the granules sit in the surface instead of rolling off.

4

Add salt if you like

A pinch of flaky salt with the sugar makes a salted maple fudge. Use it sparingly; a little goes a long way.

One 100 g box of our organic maple sugar tops several pans. The rest goes into coffee, over yogurt or in place of brown sugar in baking; our guide to maple sugar covers how to use it.

Five Variations

Keep the syrup, cream, butter and temperatures the same, and change what goes in after beating or on top.

  • Plain maple: leave out the walnuts for the smoothest fudge and the purest maple taste.
  • Pecan: ½ cup (55 g) of toasted chopped pecans in place of the walnuts.
  • Salted maple: the maple sugar top with a pinch of flaky sea salt, as above.
  • Creamier: Ricardo adds 28 g (1 oz) of chopped white chocolate and ¼ teaspoon of vanilla off the heat, without stirring, and says the chocolate makes the texture creamier without changing the flavor.
  • Double batch: four bottles of syrup, 2 cups of cream and 6 tablespoons of butter in a 23 x 33 cm (9 x 13 inch) pan. Use a bigger pot and expect it to take longer to cool.

Storing and Gifting Maple Fudge

Keep the fudge in an airtight container in the fridge, as Maple from Canada advises, with parchment between the layers. It also freezes well, according to Ricardo: wrap the squares, freeze them in a sealed container and thaw them in the fridge.

For a gift, cut the squares neatly with a knife dipped in hot water and wiped dry between cuts, then pack them in a tin lined with parchment. Add a bottle of the syrup it was made from, such as our 100 mL maple leaf bottle, and the gift explains itself.

  • Cook without stirring to 114 to 115°C (237 to 239°F), checked against boiling water first.
  • Cool untouched to 43 to 50°C, then beat 2 to 5 minutes until the shine goes.
  • Leave the crust on the sides of the pot behind when you pour.
