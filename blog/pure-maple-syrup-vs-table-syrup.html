Pure Maple Syrup vs Table Syrup: How to Tell Them Apart

Pure maple syrup has one ingredient. Table syrup often has ten or more. Here is what is actually inside the bottle most people pour on pancakes, and how to tell the difference at a glance.

By Maple Terroir May 19, 2026 9 min read

The two bottles can sit side by side on a grocery shelf and look alike, but they are different foods: one is boiled maple sap, the other is mostly corn syrup.

Pure maple syrup comes from one place: a maple tree. Producers tap the tree, collect the sap, and boil it until enough water has evaporated for the remaining liquid to thicken into syrup. The ingredient list on a bottle of pure maple syrup says "maple syrup" and nothing else. Table syrup, also sold as pancake syrup or breakfast syrup, comes from a factory. The ingredient list runs 10 or more entries deep and is led by corn syrup and high-fructose corn syrup.

This guide compares the two products on what is in the bottle, how each is made, nutrition, blood sugar, taste, price, and how a buyer can tell them apart at the grocery store. The pure maple syrup vs table syrup question has a clear answer once anyone reads the ingredient list.

How Each Is Made

Pure maple syrup. A maple producer drills a small tap into a sugar maple tree during the late winter and early spring, when daytime warming and overnight cold pulls sap up through the trunk. The sap, which leaves the tree at roughly two percent sugar, flows through tubing or buckets to an evaporator. There, the producer boils the sap until enough water evaporates for the remaining liquid to hit 66 percent sugar. That takes roughly 40 liters of sap to produce one liter of syrup. The producer bottles the syrup, applies a grade label based on color and flavor, and ships it. No additives, no chemistry, no laboratory.

Table syrup. A food manufacturer combines corn syrup and high-fructose corn syrup as the base. The blender adds water, then a thickener such as cellulose gum to give the product a syrup-like viscosity. Caramel color provides the brown tint. Salt and citric acid balance the flavor. Artificial maple flavoring, manufactured separately in a flavor lab from compounds such as sotolon and ethyl maltol, gets stirred in. Preservatives like sorbic acid and sodium benzoate extend the shelf life. The plant fills the bottles on a high-volume line and ships pallets to grocery chains.

Calorie counts run close to pure maple syrup, but the nutritional density is the difference. Table syrup is sugar dissolved in water with flavor added, not a food with measurable nutrient value.

Blood Sugar

Pure maple syrup and table syrup are both sugars, and both raise blood sugar. If you manage your blood sugar, ask your doctor or dietitian how either fits your diet.

Taste

Pure maple syrup

The flavor profile is layered: caramelized maple at the top, vanilla and butterscotch underneath, with a finish that varies by grade. Producers grade pure maple syrup by color and flavor intensity into four tiers (Golden, Amber, Dark, Very Dark). The Golden grade tastes delicate and floral, while the Very Dark grade reads as robust, with notes closer to molasses. The grade chart and what each tier pairs with is covered in detail in our guide to maple syrup grades.

Table syrup

Table syrup tastes of sweetness and added flavoring. Pure maple syrup tastes of maple, and the taste changes with the grade.

The side-by-side test

Anyone who has only ever poured table syrup will notice the change immediately on the first pour of pure maple syrup. The most common reaction is that real maple syrup is less sweet, which is partly true (the sugar content is similar but the flavor distracts from pure sweetness) and partly a function of the palate adjusting to actual flavor instead of pure sugar plus flavoring.

How to Tell at a Glance

01

Read the ingredient list

Pure maple syrup has one ingredient: maple syrup. Table syrup has 10 or more. This is the fastest test, takes three seconds, and is decisive. If the back-of-bottle list runs more than one line, the product is not pure maple syrup.

02

Check the label wording

In Canada, only syrup that meets the federal standard for maple syrup may use the name "maple syrup" on its label (Canadian Food Inspection Agency). If a brand uses qualifiers like "maple-flavored syrup," "pancake syrup," "breakfast syrup," or "table syrup," the product is not pure maple syrup.

03

Check the price

Pure maple syrup usually costs more than table syrup, but price alone does not tell you which is which. The ingredient list does.

04

Check the bottle

The bottle does not settle it either: pure maple syrup comes in glass, plastic and tins.

Single-Origin vs Blended Pure Maple Syrup

Even after a buyer chooses pure maple syrup over table syrup, a second decision waits inside the pure-maple category. Pure maple syrup can be a blend from many farms or come from one farm. Both can be Canada Grade A.

Single-origin pure maple syrup comes from one farm. The producer harvests the sap, boils it, grades it, and bottles it under their own name. The flavor reflects the specific terroir of that farm: the soil, the elevation, the maple variety, the weather of that harvest year. Different single-origin syrups taste meaningfully different from one another, the same way different vineyards produce different wines from the same grape.

Three Misconceptions About Pure Maple Syrup vs Table Syrup

"They taste the same once you put them on pancakes"

Not true. Table syrup tastes of sweetness and added flavoring. Pure maple syrup tastes of maple, and the taste changes with the grade. Pour both on pancakes and you can taste the difference.

"Table syrup is healthier because it has fewer calories"

Not true. Calorie counts run similar per tablespoon. Pure maple syrup adds manganese, zinc, calcium, potassium, and 50-plus antioxidant compounds. Table syrup adds preservatives.

"Pure maple syrup is too expensive for everyday use"

Pure Maple Syrup vs Table Syrup FAQ

Pure maple syrup contains only maple sap, boiled to reduce water. Table syrup contains corn syrup, high-fructose corn syrup, artificial maple flavor, caramel color, and preservatives. The taste, nutrition, and processing are entirely different products.

Both are mostly sugar, with similar calories per tablespoon. Pure maple syrup also contains small amounts of minerals such as manganese and zinc. Neither is a health food.

Read the ingredient list. Pure maple syrup has one ingredient: maple syrup. If the label lists corn syrup, high-fructose corn syrup, or artificial flavors, it is table syrup. Price and packaging do not settle it, because pure maple syrup is sold in glass, plastic and tins at many prices.

Producers harvest sap from maple trees, where one tree yields about 10 gallons of sap per season that boils down to roughly one quart of syrup. The 40 to 1 reduction ratio and the short seasonal harvest window create real production costs. Table syrup is industrially manufactured from corn syrup, which is far cheaper to source and blend.

Pancake syrup is not the same as maple syrup. Pancake syrup is a label often used for table syrup, which contains no actual maple sap, or only a small percentage in some brands. Pure maple syrup contains only boiled maple sap.
